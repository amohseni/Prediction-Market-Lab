Part 1

Averaging, step by step

Why the class average beat most of the class, and when it would not have. The quantity to guess is a book's page count; the true count is 400 throughout, unless you load the class's own Round 1 guesses (steps 2 and 5).

1. One guess is the truth plus an error

A person looks at the book and says a number. Their guess is the true count plus an error, and the error is as likely to be too high as too low. Press the button to draw one person's guess; the curve shows where guesses land over many people.

Where one person's guess lands

true count, 400this guess

Notice

One guess is off by about the width of the curve. Some people are close, some far, and you cannot tell which from a single guess.

What we assume

Errors are centered on the truth: no systematic tilt. That assumption is what step 4 removes.

2. Errors on both sides cancel

Now ask many people. Some errors are positive, some negative. Add the guesses up and divide: the positive and negative errors cancel, and the average lands close to the truth.

Each person's guess, and the average

distribution of one guessdistribution of the average of the guessesguess above the truthguess belowaveragetrue count

The two curves are drawn to the same height; compare their widths. Add people and the average's curve narrows; the one-guess curve does not.

Notice

Count how many people are closer to the truth than the average. Usually only a handful. The average is not the best guesser; it is better than almost every guesser.

What we assume

Each person's error is their own. If everyone had leaned the same way, there would be nothing to cancel.

3. The average's error shrinks like one over the square root of n

Ask four times as many people and the average's typical error halves. That is the whole arithmetic of the wisdom of crowds: the individual spread, divided by the square root of the crowd size.

Typical error of the average, by crowd size

simulatedspread / √n

Notice

From 1 person to 4 the error halves; from 4 to 16 it halves again; from 16 to 64 again. Early people help a lot; later people help less and less.

Where this breaks

The formula needs independent errors. Everything that links people's errors (the next step, and Part 2's cascades) slows or stops the shrinking.

4. A shared bias never cancels

Suppose everyone underestimates thick books by ten percent. Each person's error now has two parts: their own, which cancels, and a shared part, which every guess carries. The average shrinks the first part and keeps the second, however large the crowd.

Error of the average, with and without the bias

no shared biaswith the shared biasthe bias itself

Notice

With the bias, the curve flattens at the bias and stays there. A crowd of a million is no better than a crowd of a hundred. This is the ceiling: a crowd is only as wise as its errors are independent.

Where it comes from

The same news source, the same rule of thumb, the same optical illusion, the same textbook. Whatever everyone shares, the average keeps.

5. Mean, median, or trimmed mean

Galton reported the median; a reader found the mean did better. Real guesses of a count are skewed: many a little low, a few wildly high. Which average copes best depends on that skew, and you have to choose before you see the answer.

0: symmetric. Higher: a long tail of very high guesses.

One crowd's guesses, and the three averages

distribution of one guessmeanmediantrimmed mean (drop the top and bottom 10%)truth

Vertical lines: the three averages of this crowd. Outlined curves in the same colors: the distribution of each average over many crowds of this size, drawn to the same height; compare their widths and where they sit relative to the truth.

Notice

With no skew the mean wins slightly. With a long tail the mean is dragged up by a few huge guesses and the median or trimmed mean wins. The trimmed mean is the safe default when you do not know the shape.

The rule

Pick the averaging rule from what you know about the errors, before the answer is revealed. Picking it afterwards is cheating, and it is what a reader of Galton's letter did.

6. Hearing each other: three effects

Lorenz and colleagues (2011) had people guess, showed them the group's average, and let them guess again, five rounds. Everyone moves toward the average. Watch three things: the spread of the guesses, the error of the average, and whether the truth is still inside the range of guesses.

Each person's guess, round by round

averagetrutheach person

The three effects

Social influence effect: the spread collapses but the average's error does not fall. Range reduction effect: the guesses bunch on one side, so the truth ends at the edge of the range or outside it, and an outsider reading the range as a confidence interval is misled. Confidence effect: people agree, so they feel sure, though nobody got more accurate.

Why

Moving toward the average copies the shared part of everyone's error and throws away the independent parts that would have cancelled. Independence was the whole trick, and talking spends it. The fix is order: guess privately first, then talk.

7. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. The class average beat nearly every student, and no student knew the answer. Where does the accuracy come from? Say what has to be true of the students' mistakes for it to work, and what kind of mistake would defeat it no matter how many students you asked.

The accuracy comes from the structure of the errors; the people contribute only by making errors of the right kind. Each guess is the truth plus a mistake; if the mistakes are independent of each other and as likely to fall above the truth as below, they cancel when added, and the average is left near the truth. Nobody needs to know the answer; the average extracts it from the pattern of ignorance. A shared tilt (everyone anchoring on the same misleading cue) defeats it, because a mistake that everyone makes in the same direction is not cancelled by anyone, and more people only reproduce it more precisely.

2. There are two ways a crowd can be wrong: each person is noisy, or everyone leans the same way. Adding people cures one of these and does nothing for the other. Explain why, and then say whether you could tell, from the guesses alone, which kind of error you are facing.

Noise averages out because it points in different directions for different people; its effect on the average shrinks with the square root of the crowd. A shared lean points one way for everyone, so the average inherits it in full at any size. From the guesses alone you cannot tell them apart: the spread of the guesses measures the noise, but a shared lean leaves the spread untouched and moves the whole cloud. Only something outside the guesses (the truth, or an independent crowd) reveals it. That is why a large, tight, confident crowd can be wrong and give no sign of it.

3. After the class talks, the guesses converge and everyone feels surer of the group's number. In terms of the two kinds of error, what has the discussion done? Has the class learned anything it did not know before, and should an outsider trust the second number more than the first?

Discussion converts independent noise into a shared lean: people move toward what they heard, so the guesses crowd together around whatever early voices said, and the average now carries their errors. The spread shrinks and confidence rises, because confidence tracks agreement, but no new evidence entered the room; the same information was redistributed and some of it was overwritten. An outsider should trust the second number less, or at best no more: the first average pooled many independent errors, the second pools a few influential ones.

Part 2

Voting and cascades, step by step

The same idea as averaging, for yes-or-no questions: Condorcet's jury theorem, its two conditions, and what happens when people answer in turn instead of all at once.

1. One voter is right with some probability

A juror hears the evidence and votes. Call the chance they vote correctly p. Nobody is right every time; a decent juror is right more often than not. Press the button to see one juror vote ten times.

Ten votes from one person

Green: right. Red: wrong.

Notice

At p = 0.6 a single voter is wrong four times in ten. That is the raw material: individually unreliable people.

What we assume

p is the same for every question and every voter, and above one half. Steps 3 and 4 relax both.

2. Three voters and a majority

Now three jurors vote independently and the majority decides. The majority is right if all three are right, or if any two are. Add up those chances and you get the jury's accuracy. Move p and watch the jury pull ahead of, or fall behind, a single voter.

Every way three votes can come out

Notice

Above one half, the jury beats the voter; at exactly one half they tie; below one half the jury is worse. The majority amplifies whatever tendency the voters have.

The formula

Three voters: p³ + 3p²(1−p). It is the same arithmetic as averaging: a sum of independent draws lands on the side that each draw leans toward.

3. Condorcet's jury theorem

Keep adding independent voters. The number who vote correctly is a sum of coin flips, each landing "right" with probability p. Its average is p times n, and its spread grows only like the square root of n, so for large n the count almost never falls below half. That is the theorem (Condorcet, 1785).

Number of voters who are right, out of n (exact probabilities)

Each bar is the chance that exactly that many of the n voters are right. Green bars: more than half are right, so the majority is right; red bars: fewer than half. The green area is the jury accuracy, and it is what grows as n grows. The bars themselves spread over more values as n grows, but the spread grows slower than the center moves away from half.

Jury accuracy as n grows

Notice

The bars pile up to the right of the half line when p > 0.5, and the pile gets narrower relative to its position as n grows. Set p to 0.45: the same narrowing now pushes the jury toward always being wrong.

Two conditions

Competence: p above one half on this question, which nothing in the theorem checks. Independence: each vote is its own draw. The next two steps break independence in two different ways.

4. Copies of one signal

Suppose a share of the jurors did not look at the evidence themselves but took their view from one shared source (the same newspaper, the same expert). Those votes are copies. They count in the tally, but they carry one draw, not many.

Jury accuracy as n grows, with and without copying

all independentwith copyingthe source's own accuracy, p

Notice

With enough copiers the jury's accuracy stops rising and settles near p: the crowd is exactly as good as the one source it copied. Adding voters adds copies, not evidence. This is the same ceiling as the shared bias in Part 1.

Where it comes from

One poll everyone has read, one pundit, one rumor, one training set. The jury theorem's independence condition is the demanding one, and it fails quietly.

5. Voting in turn: the cascade

Now the jurors announce their votes one at a time, and each hears everyone before them. Each has a private signal, A or B, right with probability p. A sensible juror counts the informative votes so far. Once two more people have said A than B, their own signal cannot outweigh that, so they say A whatever their signal, and so does everyone after them. Step through one line.

Twenty people in a line: small letter is the private signal, the circle is what they say

Press New line.

Notice

Dashed circles are people who ignored their own signal. Once the dashed circles start, no new evidence enters: the rest of the line is copying, and the line's answer was decided by its first two or three people.

Why it is rational

Each follower is doing the right thing given what they know. The crowd is wrong because the order of speaking turned twenty signals into two. Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch (1992) called this an informational cascade.

6. Cascades do not get better with size

Run thousands of lines. The chance the line ends on the wrong answer is (1−p)² divided by p² + (1−p)², and it does not depend on how many people are in the line. Compare that with the same people voting independently: Condorcet's curve goes to one; the cascade's stays flat.

Chance the crowd is wrong, by how they answer

in turn (cascade), exactindependent majority of the same n peopleone person alone

Notice

At p = 0.6, the cascade crowd is wrong 31% of the time whether the line has 5 people or 500; the independent majority of 51 is wrong 7% of the time. Same people, same signals; only the order of speaking changed.

The fix, and the fragility

Collect the signals before anyone speaks (secret ballots, private guesses first). Cascades are also fragile: one person who is known to have better information, or a little public evidence, can flip the whole line. That is why fads end suddenly.

7. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. Majority rule makes a crowd of slightly-better-than-chance voters almost certainly right and a crowd of slightly-worse-than-chance voters almost certainly wrong. Explain, in terms of what each added voter does, why one rule produces both results, and what this means for how much a lopsided vote should move you.

Each independent voter adds a small push in the direction of their tendency, and the pushes accumulate while the noise around them averages away, so a large crowd ends up wherever its typical member leans, with near certainty. Majority rule amplifies the tendency; it does not know whether the tendency is toward the truth. A lopsided vote is therefore strong evidence that the voters lean that way, and evidence about the truth only to the extent you already believe the voters lean toward it. The size of the majority is a fact about the crowd; what it says about the world depends on what you already believe about the crowd.

2. Twelve jurors who all formed their view from one expert's report are, for the purposes of the theorem, a jury of what size? Generalize: what is the right way to count a crowd, and why is counting heads the wrong way?

A jury of one, with eleven echoes. The theorem counts independent pieces of evidence, and twelve votes that trace to one report carry one piece. Head-counting treats every vote as new evidence, which is exactly the assumption that shared sources break. The right count is the number of distinct signals behind the votes: a crowd of a thousand reading one newspaper is a crowd of one on every question that newspaper answered for them, and the vote will be as confident, and as fallible, as the paper.

3. In the sequential vote every juror is rational and nobody's evidence changed, yet the group does worse. Where did the information go? Say why a cascade is at once self-reinforcing and fragile, and what that implies about a unanimous room in which people spoke in turn.

Once the first two announcements agree, the third juror's best guess is to follow them even against their own signal; and since everyone knows that, the third vote carries no information about the third signal, nor does any vote after it. The private evidence still exists, but it never reaches the public record. The cascade is self-reinforcing because each follower makes following look more justified, and fragile because the whole chain rests on two signals: one public piece of contrary evidence can flip it. So a unanimous room that spoke in turn is weak evidence, however many people are in it; the order of speaking produced the unanimity, and the facts had little to do with it.

Part 3

Prediction markets, step by step

Six steps. Each one adds a single idea to the one before. The traders are the same people as in the book experiment: each holds one noisy piece of evidence about a yes-or-no question.

1. A price is a probability

A share that pays $1 if the answer is YES, and nothing otherwise, is worth exactly your probability of YES. If the share sells for less than that, buy; if for more, sell. So the price at which nobody wants to trade is the market's probability.

What one share is worth to you, against what it costs

Notice

Your expected profit per share is belief minus price. It is positive on one side of the price and negative on the other, so a trader with a different probability from the market always has a trade to make. The price where you stop is your own probability.

Where this breaks

A price equals a probability only for a contract that pays a fixed amount on a clean yes-or-no event. If the event is vague ("the economy improves"), or if traders care about the money differently in the two outcomes, the price drifts from the probability (Manski 2006).

2. One trader moves the price

Somebody has to sell you the share. In this market an automated market maker always quotes a price and raises it as you buy. How much each dollar moves the price is set by one number, the liquidity.

The rule (Hanson's logarithmic market scoring rule): the price is a smooth function of how many YES and NO shares are out; buying b shares of YES, where b is the liquidity, moves the log-odds of the price by exactly one.

Price after buying, starting from 0.50

this liquidityliquidity 5 and 100 for comparison

Notice

Low liquidity: a few dollars swing the price, so one confident trader can move it a long way, and so can one fool. High liquidity: the price barely moves, so it takes a lot of money to say anything. Liquidity is the market's stubbornness.

Where this breaks

The market maker loses money to informed traders by design; that loss is the price of the forecast, and it is capped by the liquidity. A market with no maker and few traders (a thin market) has jumpy prices and long silences.

3. Evidence adds up

Now let traders arrive one at a time. Each holds one private signal that is right with some probability. A trader who trusts the price treats it as a summary of everyone before them, adds their own evidence on top, and trades to the new probability. Watch the price walk.

The price after each trader

pricep*: the forecast from all signalspoll: average of the traders' own beliefsthe answer

Notice

Each YES signal moves the price up by the same step in log-odds, each NO signal down. After everyone has traded, the price is the probability you would compute from all the signals at once: p*. Nobody in the market knows all the signals; the price does.

Where this breaks

The walk only works because each trader adds their evidence once and then trusts the price. A trader who keeps betting their own belief drags the price back toward it (step 4). A trader who cannot afford to move the price by a full step leaves evidence outside the market (step 2).

4. Averaging beliefs is not adding evidence

A poll asks everyone their probability and averages the answers. Each answer reflects one signal, so the average sits near 0.6 or 0.4 no matter how many people you ask. The market instead adds the signals, so its price becomes sharp. Run many questions and score both.

1: add your evidence once, then trust the price. 0: always bet toward your own belief.

Forecast error (Brier score: 0 is perfect, 0.25 is saying 50% every time)

Notice

With full trust in the price the market matches p*, the best any forecaster could do with these signals. The poll is barely better than the prior. Lower the trust and the market slides toward the poll: a tug of war between private beliefs ends near their average.

Where this breaks

Forecasting tournaments fix the poll's weakness by extremizing: pushing the average probability away from 0.5 to undo the fact that each person holds only part of the evidence. Trusting the price is the market's version of the same move, and step 5 shows its cost.

5. Manipulation, and what it costs

A manipulator does not care about the answer; they want the price at a target. They buy until it gets there. Everyone who trades after them sees a price that contains a lie. What happens next depends on whether the other traders treat the push as evidence or as a bargain.

The price with and without the manipulator (30 traders, signals right 60% of the time)

with manipulatorsame traders, no manipulatorp*the answer

Notice

The push costs the manipulator money on average and hands it to the traders who bet against it. With some distrust of the price, the market pulls part of the way back, and the traders who corrected it end richer than they would have been without the manipulator: the push is a subsidy to informed trading (Hanson and Oprea 2009). Raise the liquidity and the same push moves the price less.

Where this breaks

If every trader treats every price move as evidence (trust 1), the push is believed and stays in the price until the manipulator runs out of money. The defenses are depth (liquidity), breadth (more traders with their own evidence), and traders who hold beliefs of their own. Full distrust is not a defense: it destroys the adding-up of step 3.

6. Shared error: the ceiling

Everything so far assumed each trader's signal was an independent look at the world. Now let some traders hold copies of one shared signal: the same poll, the same pundit, the same rumor. Copies are one piece of evidence, but the market counts each copy as new.

Forecast error as the shared share rises

marketp*: best possible, counting the shared signal oncepollprior (0.25)

Notice

Two things happen. The best possible forecast gets worse, because there is less distinct evidence: that is the ceiling, and no mechanism gets above it. And the market falls below the ceiling, and eventually below the prior, because it adds the shared signal many times over and becomes confidently wrong whenever that one signal is wrong.

Where this breaks

This is the failure that survived 2008 and 2016: markets, polls, and experts sharing the same model, the same data, the same assumption. A crowd is only as wise as its errors are independent. The averaging crowd hits the same ceiling (Part 1); the voting crowd too (Part 2).

7. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. What makes a market price a probability rather than just a number? Say what must be true of the traders for that reading to hold, and describe a market in which the price is nobody's probability.

Disagreement is costly. A trader who thinks the event is more likely than the price can buy shares that are, by their lights, underpriced, and their buying moves the price toward their belief; the price comes to rest where nobody with money and a belief wants to move it. That reading requires traders who are willing to bet, who have something to bet with, and whose beliefs are not all the same misreading. A market with one wealthy trader and many poor ones, or a thin market where the only recent trade was a manipulator's push, shows a price that records one person's wager; no crowd's probability is in it. The price is a probability only to the degree that the incentive to correct it is spread across people with independent information.

2. The poll and the market use the same people with the same evidence, yet the market lands near the best possible forecast and the poll does not. Explain the difference in what each does with a single piece of evidence, and why the market's method only works if traders believe the price.

A poll asks each person for their belief and averages; each belief reflects one signal, so the average of many one-signal beliefs stays near what one signal justifies. A market asks each person to act on the difference between their belief and the current price; if they treat the price as the evidence already collected and add their own on top, each trade contributes an increment, and increments add up to the forecast that uses every signal. That only works if traders believe the price: a trader who ignores it keeps pulling the price back to their single signal, undoing the accumulation. Adding evidence requires taking the sum so far as given.

3. Trusting the price is what lets the market add up evidence and what lets a manipulator's push, or a shared rumor, stay in the price. Is that trade-off fundamental or can a clever trader escape it? What does the market need in order to recover, and who pays for the recovery?

From the price alone a trader cannot tell an evidence-driven move from a push: both look like a price change. So any rule that absorbs price moves as information will absorb the push, and any rule that resists price moves will also resist real evidence. The trade-off lives in the trader's rule and is not escaped by cleverness about the price; it is escaped only by information from outside the price. The market recovers when traders with their own evidence and their own money are present to sell to the manipulator at inflated prices, and the manipulator pays them: a push is a subsidy to whoever is informed enough to take the other side. A market with no such traders, or too few, keeps the push.

One crowd, every mechanism

3. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. Every mechanism sits below p*, and none can climb above it. What is p*, why is it a ceiling, and what does the ceiling say about the difference between a better mechanism and better evidence?

p* is the forecast of someone who saw every distinct signal in the crowd and combined them correctly; it is the most the evidence can support. A mechanism only decides how much of that evidence reaches the answer, so the best it can do is lose nothing. Mechanism design is therefore about minimizing loss, and its gains are bounded: once a mechanism is near p*, the only way up is more or better evidence, which no voting rule or market can create. The question for any crowd is first how high the ceiling is, and only then how far below it the mechanism lands.

2. Each mechanism loses information in its own way. For the secret ballot, the sequential vote, talking first, the poll, and the market, name the step at which evidence is lost or counted twice, and say which of those losses a designer can remove and which only the people can.

The secret ballot loses the strength of each signal (a weak and a strong YES count the same) but keeps every signal; the sequential vote loses every signal after the cascade starts; talking first overwrites some people's signals with their neighbors' and counts the neighbors' twice; the poll keeps every belief but averages instead of adding, so the total never grows past one signal's worth; the market keeps the adding but leaks whenever traders distrust the price or absorb a push. A designer can fix the order of speaking, the secrecy of the vote, and the rule for combining answers. The designer cannot fix shared signals: when half the crowd holds one copy, every mechanism's ceiling falls together, and no procedure restores the missing independence.

3. If you could change one feature of a crowd, which would raise every mechanism's accuracy at once? Say why that feature is hard to arrange in practice (think of a classroom, a jury, an election, a scientific field), and what institutions exist to protect it.

Independence of evidence: people drawing on different sources rather than copies of one. It raises the ceiling itself, so every mechanism gains. It is hard to arrange because people share sources by default (the same news, the same textbook, the same expert, the same conversation), and sharing feels like diligence rather than a loss. Institutions that protect it include sequestered juries, secret ballots, independent replication, pre-registration, blind review, and the practice of writing one's view down before hearing others'. Each is a device for keeping signals distinct long enough to be counted.

Part 5

Better than the mean

Three aggregation rules that repair a failure the plain average cannot: weighting by track record, extremizing, and the surprisingly popular answer. Each fixes one thing and has a condition of its own.

1. Weight by track record

If voters differ in accuracy, the best rule gives each vote a weight that grows with the voter's accuracy (the log-odds of being right). But the rule needs to know who is accurate. Slide the noise in that knowledge from none to total and watch the advantage disappear.

Voters' accuracies are spread evenly from 0.6 minus this to 0.6 plus this.

Accuracy of three rules as knowledge of the voters degrades

simple majorityweighted by estimated accuracyselect crowd: majority of the five thought best

Notice

With accurate track records, weighting beats the simple majority and a select crowd of five beats the whole crowd (Mannes, Soll and Larrick 2014 found this with real forecasters). As the track records get noisier, weighting degrades to the majority and the select crowd falls below it: choosing five at random is worse than using everyone.

The condition

Weighting helps only when you know something true about who is accurate. Most real crowds have no track record, and an assumed one (credentials, confidence, seniority) is often noise.

2. Extremizing

A poll averages probabilities that each rest on one signal, so the average sits near 0.6 when the evidence, taken together, says 0.95. Extremizing pushes the average away from one half by a fixed factor in log-odds. Find the factor that works, then add shared error and watch it overshoot.

Brier score of the extremized poll against the factor

independent signalshalf the forecasters share one signalp* for each case

Notice

With independent signals the best factor is about the number of forecasters: the poll reaches p* when you multiply its log-odds by n, which is the same as adding up the evidence (Part 3, step 4). With shared error the best factor is much smaller, and the factor that was right for independent signals is now confidently wrong. Forecasting tournaments extremize by factors of 1.5 to 3, not n, for this reason.

The condition

Extremizing assumes the forecasters hold separate evidence. The more they share, the less you should push.

3. The surprisingly popular answer

Is Philadelphia the capital of Pennsylvania? Most people say yes. Ask a second question: what share of people will say yes? Those who know it is Harrisburg also know most people will say Philadelphia, so they predict a high yes share. The yes-sayers predict a high yes share too. So the no answer is more common than anyone predicted: surprisingly popular, and correct (Prelec, Seung and McCoy 2017).

Votes against predicted votes

actual share saying each answeraverage predicted share

Notice

The majority is wrong whenever the knowers are under half, and the surprisingly popular rule is right as long as some people know, because the knowers also know what the misled will say. It fails when nobody knows (slide the share to zero) and when the misled happen to predict correctly.

What it fixes

This is the one rule that can beat a majority sharing the same wrong belief, which is the failure the mean, the vote, and the market all have. Its price is a second question and the assumption that the people who know the answer also know the crowd.

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. Weighting by track record can beat the plain average and can lose to it. What does the weighted rule require that the plain average does not, and why does the plain average stay a sensible default even when you believe some people are better than others?

The weighted rule requires knowing who is better, and how much. Those weights are themselves estimates with error, and an error in a weight is an error applied to every forecast that person makes. When track records are long and the task is stable, the weights are accurate and the rule gains; when records are short or the task changes, the weights are mostly noise and the rule amplifies it, so the plain average, which cannot be fooled about who is good because it does not ask, wins. Equal weighting is the choice that is never badly wrong, which is what a default should be.

2. Extremizing says the crowd's average probability is too timid. Timid relative to what? Explain why the right amount of extremizing depends on how much the forecasters' information overlaps, so that the aggregator has to hold a theory about the crowd as well as its answers.

Timid relative to the forecast that treats each forecaster's information as separate evidence and adds it. An average of probabilities built on separate evidence understates the total, because averaging cannot rise above the most confident input while adding can. But if the forecasters all read the same report, their probabilities are one piece of evidence repeated, and the average is already right; extremizing it would count that report many times over. The correct factor is therefore a function of overlap, which the answers alone do not reveal. The aggregator must know, or assume, how the crowd came to its views, and the same number can be too timid or too bold depending on that fact.

3. The surprisingly popular rule asks people a question about other people. Why can that question reveal a truth the majority vote hides? Describe the asymmetry between the people who know and the people who are misled that the rule relies on, and a case where the asymmetry fails.

People who know the truth usually also know the common misconception, so they predict that most others will give the popular wrong answer. People who hold the misconception do not know it is a misconception, so they predict that others agree with them. The result is that the true answer gets more votes than the crowd predicted it would, and that gap, rather than the vote count, marks it. The rule rests on knowledge being asymmetric in this way: the informed can model the uninformed but not the reverse. It fails when nobody knows the truth, so there is no one to be surprised, or when the misled are themselves aware that opinion is split and predict the split correctly.

Part 6

Scoring and calibration

Every lesson reports a Brier score. This one says what it is, why it rewards honesty, and how to tell an overconfident forecaster from an honest one.

1. The Brier score

A forecast is a probability p. When the answer arrives, score it by the squared distance between p and what happened (1 for YES, 0 for NO). Zero is perfect. Saying 0.5 every time scores 0.25. Saying 1 and being wrong scores 1.

Notice

A confident wrong forecast is punished much more than a hedged one is rewarded: the curves are steep near the ends. The score is a penalty for both wrongness and overconfidence, in one number.

Why squared

Not for elegance. The next step shows that squaring is what makes the score honest.

2. Why honesty pays

Suppose you believe the chance of YES is really some value. What forecast minimizes your expected Brier score? Exactly that value: the score is proper, which means the best strategy is to report what you believe. Compare with an absolute-distance score, which tells you to exaggerate.

Expected score for each forecast you could report

Brier (squared): lowest at your beliefabsolute distance: lowest at 0 or 1

Notice

Under the absolute score, someone who believes 0.7 should say 1.0. Under the Brier score, saying 1.0 costs more than it gains. A market pays with money the way the Brier score pays with points: the trader who bets more than their belief warrants loses on average.

The point

Choosing the scoring rule is choosing what you get told. A rule that is not proper collects exaggerations, and averaging exaggerations is not averaging beliefs.

3. Calibration

A forecaster is calibrated if, of the things they call 70% likely, about 70% happen. Simulate a forecaster who sees the true chances but reports them pushed toward the extremes (overconfident) or toward the middle (underconfident), and read their calibration curve.

Back to the crowd

The Lorenz experiment's confidence effect (Part 1, step 6) is a calibration failure: after talking, people's confidence rose and their accuracy did not. Calibration is how you catch that in the wild.

4. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. Why does the choice of scoring rule decide what a reported probability means? Say what makes the Brier score proper, and what happens to the meaning of a crowd's average forecast when the rule is not.

A forecaster reports whatever the score rewards. Under a proper rule the report that maximizes expected score is the forecaster's actual belief, so the number can be read as a probability. The Brier score is proper because its expected value, for someone who believes the event has probability p, is lowest exactly when they report p: any shading toward 0 or 1 raises the expected penalty. Under a rule that is not proper, such as absolute distance, the best report is 0 or 1 whichever way the forecaster leans, so the reports become votes; their average then measures the share of people leaning YES, which is a fact about the crowd, and cannot be read as anyone's probability of the event.

2. A forecaster who says 0.5 about everything can never be badly wrong. Why is that the wrong idea of safety under a proper score, and what are the two separate things a good forecaster is being rewarded for?

Saying 0.5 guarantees a mediocre score on every question, and a proper score charges for mediocrity on every question: a forecaster who is confident and usually right beats the hedger even after paying for the occasional confident miss. The score rewards two things that can be separated: calibration, meaning that events called 70% likely happen about 70% of the time, and resolution, meaning that the forecaster sorts events into groups that differ in how often they happen. The hedger is perfectly calibrated and has no resolution; a good forecaster has both, and the Brier score charges for the lack of either.

3. Can you tell whether a single forecast of 90% was overconfident? Say what a track record can show that one forecast cannot, and what it would take to tell a calibrated forecaster from a lucky one.

A single forecast cannot be overconfident or underconfident in any checkable way: the event happens or it does not, and either outcome is consistent with 90% being the right number. Calibration is a property of a record: across many 90% calls, about nine in ten should come true, and a forecaster whose 90% calls come true six times in ten is overconfident as a matter of fact, whatever any one of those calls deserved. Separating calibration from luck takes enough forecasts, across enough questions, that the observed rate could not easily have arisen by chance, which is why forecasting tournaments score hundreds of questions and why a pundit's famous correct call tells you almost nothing.

Part 7

Talking without herding

Talking can add evidence or destroy independence, and the design of the conversation decides which. Four designs, one crowd, and the order of speaking as its own experiment.

1. Four ways to run the room

The same crowd decides a yes-or-no question under four procedures: a secret ballot; answering in turn out loud; discussing opinions with neighbors, then voting; and telling neighbors your evidence, then voting. The first two you know. The last two differ in what gets passed around.

Share of questions the group answers correctly

procedurep*, the ceiling

Notice

Sharing evidence does no harm and, with wide enough circles, does no better than the ballot either: the ballot already pools every signal. Sharing opinions costs a little at low conformity and more at high conformity. Answering in turn costs the most. What gets passed around matters more than how much talking there is.

Why evidence-sharing is safe

A signal repeated is still one signal; a vote repeated becomes a new vote. Discussion that reports observations ("I saw a red ball") adds nothing false; discussion that reports conclusions ("I think A") lets one conclusion be counted many times.

2. Who speaks first

When people answer in turn, the first two or three decide the outcome. So the order matters. Give the crowd unequal accuracy and try three orders: random, most accurate first, least accurate first.

How often the line ends on the right answer

Notice

Most-accurate-first nearly closes the gap to the ballot; least-accurate-first can make the line worse than one good person. Seniority-first, the usual order in meetings, is whichever of these seniority happens to be.

The design rule

If people must speak in turn, collect written answers first, or let the person with the best track record go first. Hidden-profile experiments (Stasser and Titus 1985) show groups mostly discuss what everyone already knows; asking for evidence rather than conclusions is the fix for that too.

3. Check yourself

Write a few sentences on each before you open the answer. The questions ask for the logic of the lesson, so a good answer explains why, and says what the claim depends on.

1. The same conversation can make a group wiser or dumber. What is the difference between what gets passed around in the two cases, and why does passing a conclusion destroy information that passing the evidence behind it preserves?

In the good case people pass evidence: observations, reasons, sources that others did not have, and each listener combines them with their own. In the bad case people pass conclusions: votes, estimates, confidence, and each listener replaces or shades their own signal with someone else's. A conclusion compresses several signals into one bit and discards the rest, so when it is copied the group ends up counting a few signals many times and the others not at all. Evidence, passed intact, can be combined with what the listener knows and counted once. Whether talk adds or destroys depends on which of the two is moving through the room.

2. The order of speaking changes the group's accuracy as much as the group's competence does. Explain why the front of a sequence matters so much more than the back, and what that implies for how a meeting, a seminar, or a hiring committee should be run.

In a sequence the early speakers set a public record that later speakers rationally defer to; after two or three agreeing voices, the rest add little whatever they know. Accuracy at the front enters the group's decision; accuracy at the back is wasted. So the people with the most relevant evidence should speak first, or, better, everyone should commit privately before anyone speaks, so that there is no front. For a meeting that means written positions before discussion; for a seminar, students answer before the professor reacts; for a committee, the chair speaks last. The usual order, senior people first, is the one most likely to waste the room.

3. Design a decision procedure for a group of ten that keeps all the evidence and removes the herding. Then say what it costs, and where it will still fail if the ten share a source.

Each member writes down their estimate and the evidence for it before any discussion; the written evidence (not the estimates) is circulated or read aloud; each member then revises privately; the final estimates are combined by a fixed rule chosen in advance, such as the median or a trimmed mean. The costs are time, the loss of the quick consensus that discussion produces, some discomfort for people whose written view was unpopular, and a reduction in the authority of senior members, who can no longer set the tone. The procedure still fails if the ten share a source: the private estimates will agree because they trace to the same report, the written evidence will be ten copies of one document, and the combined estimate will be as wrong as that document, with the full confidence of a careful procedure behind it.
